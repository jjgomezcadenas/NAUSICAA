\begin{abstract}
ITACA (Ion Tracking Apparatus with CMOS ASICs) is a proposed new detector to search for neutrinoless double beta decay (\bbonu) events in a future, (multi) ton scale, ultra-low background, high pressure xenon TPC. The crucial innovation introduced by ITACA is the measurement of both the electron and the ion tracks produced in the xenon gas in response to ionizing particles. The readout of the ion track involves the use of electronics able to measure the charge carried by the positive xenon ions arriving at the cathode. We propose a design based on bare metal pads (TopMetal) coupled to CMOS electronics, which we call NAUSICA (Non-Amplified Ultra-Slow Ion CMOS ASIC). 

This paper presents a conceptual design of NAUSICA and a simplified model of its response to ion tracks. We use a Geant4-based simulation of ITACA to generate \bbonu\ events uniformly in the fiducial volume of the detector. The ions produced by each track drift to the cathode where they are focused towards the metal electrodes. We calculate the distribution of ion arrival positions and times after drift, diffusion and focusing and the current induced on each collection pad. A charge-sensitive amplifier (CSA) computes the voltage produced as this current is integrated on a feedback capacitor with a finite discharge time. We then add electronic noise and digitise the voltage to obtain the digitised signal from each pad. The combined response of all the pads with signals above a noise-dependent threshold allows the reconstruction of the track. We show that the track can be
resolved above the noise, keeping its characteristic topology.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The next generation of neutrinoless double-beta decay (\bbonu) experiments, aiming to establish if the neutrino is its own antiparticle, will attempt to explore lifetimes for a putative decay, $T_{1/2} > 10^{28}$~yr. This ambitious goal will require (multi) ton target masses of a double-beta decaying isotope and essentially zero backgrounds~\cite{Gomez-Cadenas:2023vca}. 
High Pressure Xenon TPCs (HPXe)~\cite{Gomez-Cadenas:2019ges} are one of the leading technologies for such a generation of large, virtually background-free \bbonu\ detectors. In particular, the NEXT collaboration is currently operating the NEXT-100 apparatus \cite{NEXT:2025yqw} at the Canfranc Underground Laboratory, a large scale detector which can hold $\sim100$~kg of xenon at 15~bar, in a high pressure time projection chamber of roughly 1~m$^3$ volume. NEXT has already demonstrated a FWHM energy resolution of $(0.93 \pm 0.02)$\% at \Qbb~\cite{NEXT:2025ozn}. 

An HPXe TPC provides a distinctive topological signature that enables identification of events with two emitted electrons. A \bbonu\ candidate is defined as a single, continuous track, with no detached energy deposits and fully contained inside the fiducial volume. The energy of the track must be close to \Qbb\, typically within a ROI of $\pm 1\sigma$.  This ``single-track'' requirement suppresses nearly all backgrounds, leaving only single-electron events with energies near \Qbb, such as those arising from photoelectric interactions (without an accompanying X-ray satellite) of the 2.448~MeV $\gamma$ ray of \BI\ and the 2.615~MeV $\gamma$ ray of \TL, as well as from beta decays of $^{137}$Xe created by cosmogenic activation. In addition, requiring large local energy deposits (``blobs'') near the two endpoints of the track permits an additional suppression of single electron backgrounds that make it into the ROI. The effectiveness of this topological discrimination has been demonstrated by the NEXT collaboration~\cite{Ferrario:2015kta, Ferrario:2019kwg, NEXT:2021vzd, Kekic2021, NEXT:2021pjq}. 

However, the performance of the topological signature in an HPXeEL TPC is limited by diffusion and by electroluminescent blurring. As ionisation electrons drift through the dense gas, they undergo diffusion, producing a charge cloud whose transverse size increases with the drift length. In addition, EL amplification introduces further smearing, as the EL photons are emitted isotropically. 

In two recent papers~\cite{Gomez-Cadenas:2025ect, itaca2026}, we proposed a new approach to improving the topological signature, while preserving EL amplification, needed for energy resolution. The method relies on recording two complementary tracks for each event: the \emph{electron track}---the established signature in an HPXeEL TPC---and the \emph{ion track}, formed by the positive ions left along the original particle trajectory. The ion track exhibits diffusion at the millimetre level and is free from EL-induced smearing, providing a dramatic boost to topological discrimination.
The Ion Tracking Apparatus with CMOS ASICs (\textsc{ITACA})~\cite{itaca2026} is a proposed high pressure xenon TPC based on this principle. The ions are read out using bare metal pads (TopMetal) coupled to CMOS electronics.

In this paper we present a conceptual design of NAUSICA (Non-Amplified Ultra-Slow Ion CMOS ASIC), our proposed readout for ITACA. Our study~\cite{itaca2026} showed that the topological signature that allows distinguishing between signal (\bbonu\ events which produce two electrons) and single-electron backgrounds could increase the rejection power of the HPXe technology by at least one order of magnitude, provided that the ion track could be reconstructed with a spatial resolution at the level of
ion diffusion ($\sigma \sim 1$--$2$~mm). Here we show that the proposed NAUSICA readout can preserve the resolution at the level dictated by the diffusion, thus guaranteeing the expected enhancement of the topology discrimination power.

\section{The ITACA Detector}
\label{sec:itaca_det}

The pressure of operation in ITACA is chosen as a tradeoff between mass --- which increases with pressure --- and track topology, which is easier to resolve at lower pressure. Other considerations include ion drift velocity, which decreases approximately inversely with pressure at fixed electric field, and electron lifetime, which tends to improve at lower pressure. Overall, 15 bar is a good compromise. Using electroluminescence to amplify the electron track signal guarantees excellent energy resolution. Fiducialisation of the event requires the measurement of $t_0$, provided by primary scintillation. Both requirements are essential for a \bbonu\ experiment.

 Choosing the detector dimensions implies a tradeoff between the diameter and the length of the TPC. A major design goal is to minimise the transverse diffusion $\sigma_T$ of ions, which in the thermal limit is simply:
 %
 \begin{equation}
\sigma_T =\sqrt{\tfrac{2k_BT}{q_e}\tfrac{L}{E}} 
\label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
%
where $L$ is the ion drift distance, $E$ the drift field, $T$ the gas temperature, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant and $q_e$ the elementary charge. Equation~\ref{eq:drift} favours shorter $L$ and larger $E$. Figure~\ref{fig:sigmatvsl} shows $\sigma_T$ for three different drift fields. For a TPC drift length of 150~cm, the average $\sigma_T$ is of the order of \SI{1}{mm}, one order of magnitude smaller than the corresponding diffusion for electrons in pure xenon.

Another important design factor is the voltages in the TPC electrodes. All sensors (the CMOS reading the ions and the SiPMs reading the EL light) operate best at ground, but the drifting of electrons and ions requires a constant field in the TPC, and the generation of EL light requires an additional, higher field, in the EL region. 

The cathode is instrumented with an Ion Focusing Grid (IFG) which focuses the incoming ions into the readout pads of the CMOS detector. The upper electrode of the IFG is at a voltage $V_c = \SI{8}{kV}$. The voltage on the anode side of the TPC depends on the drift length and the drift field. A good compromise between minimising diffusion and keeping voltage at the anode reasonable is to choose a drift field of \SI{200}{V/cm}, which results in \SI{38}{kV} at the first anode electrode, and \SI{54}{kV} at the second (with \SI{16}{kV} across the EL gap). 
 
 Once the length of the TPC is defined, the diameter of the TPC defines the fiducial mass that can be achieved. At 15 bar, choosing $D= 320$ cm, results in a fiducial mass slightly above one ton, $M_{fid} = 1050$ kg. 
 
 A large diameter TPC requires instrumenting both the EL side (with SiPMs) and the cathode side (with top metal devices). 
  Previous work involving the detection of
 ions for \bbonu\ experiments \cite{Nygren_2018, mei2020topmetalcmosdirectcharge} has quantified the challenge of instrumenting a large surface with CMOS detectors, which appears, nonetheless, feasible \cite{You2021}. The ITACA paper~\cite{itaca2026} proposes a movable ion detector, the Magnetically Actuated Rotor System (MARS). The NAUSICA readout considered here could be used as part of MARS or to instrument the full cathode.  Table~\ref{tab:itaca_params} shows the main parameters of a 1-ton ITACA detector.

\subsection{Principle of operation}
\label{sec:pof}

Consider a putative \bbonu\ event occurring in the middle of the TPC. Two electrons, with a total energy of \Qbb\ (\SI{2458}{keV}), propagate through the dense noble gas, ionising it. Primary scintillation is recorded and a fast trigger system (FTS) records the start-of-the-event $t_0$. Then a fast measurement of the event energy is performed, accepting the event only if its energy lies within a pre-determined ROI (selected to be $\pm 1\sigma$ around \Qbb). Events outside the ROI are rejected and no ion readout is attempted.

Since the event that we are considering is a \bbonu\ decay, the FTS will likely trigger, activating a fast reconstruction of the topology and event barycenter, $(x_i, y_i, z_i)$. The coordinates $(x_i,y_i)$ predict the landing position of the ion cloud at the cathode, while $z_i$, together with $t_0$ and the ion drift velocity, determines the arrival time of its barycenter ($t_i$). 

     
The \xetp\ ions reach the cathode on a time scale of seconds. In our specific example, with the event at the midpoint of the TPC, they need to drift \SI{75}{cm} at a velocity of \SI{10}{cm/s}, requiring \SI{7.5}{s}. 
The typical longitudinal extension of the ion track is of the order of \SI{10}{cm}, and thus about \SI{1}{s} is needed to collect the ions. 

Notice that the electron track and the ion track are detected in \emph{delayed coincidence}. The ion track is intercepted precisely at the right time and in the right place, thus minimising the impact of spurious coincidences. At the expected trigger rate (dominated by backgrounds in the ROI), pile-up during the $\sim$\SI{15}{s} maximum ion drift time is negligible.


\section{The NAUSICA concept}
\label{sec:nausica}

Reading the arriving ions (without amplification) requires exposed metal electrodes connected to charge-sensitive amplifiers (CSAs). Our proposed  \emph{Non-Amplified Ultra-Slow Ion CMOS ASIC} (NAUSICA) uses TopMetal CMOS technology~\cite{An2016TopmetalII} to implement this concept, following Refs.~\cite{Nygren_2018, mei2020topmetalcmosdirectcharge}. There is an important difference, however, with respect to these works, which attempted to design a system able to provide simultaneously good energy resolution and a good measurement of the track topology using ions. Alas, both requirements impose constraints that interfere with each other. Good energy resolution requires optimizing ion counting and minimizing noise. Good topological reconstruction demands pixels separated by a small pitch (e.g., of the order of diffusion, $\sim 2$ mm), which in turn multiplies the number of pixels and adds to the overall noise. The advantage of ITACA is that energy resolution is measured with excellent precision by electroluminescence, and thus the ion detector can prioritize the reconstruction of the topology. NAUSICA is designed with this goal in mind. 

The principle of operation is as follows. The ions arrive at the metal pad over a time of order 1 second, taking a 10 cm extent along the drift direction as an extreme case. The resolution in $z$ should be of the order of the resolution in the transverse coordinates ($\sigma_z = 1-2$ mm). Since the drift velocity of the ions is $\sim 10$ cm/s, this translates into slicing the charge in buckets of $10-20$ ms, which implies a framed readout (about 50 frames of 20 ms each). The mean number of ions per pad in those buckets helps determine the feedback capacitance of the CSA.

\subsection{Charge collection and focusing}

The pad dimensions and pitch need to be chosen balancing the desired spatial resolution with the need for an acceptable signal-to-noise (S/N) per pad. Larger pads increase the collection but also the input capacitance, thus the need to focus the incoming ions in an area as small as possible. The left panel of Fig. \ref{fig:nausicaa} shows a schematic of the Ion Focusing Grid (IFG), which does this job.  We estimate the focusing requirement by comparing the electric flux through one pixel cell with that through its hole. Taking uniform fields in the drift region and inside the hole, full flux collection requires
\[
E_h\,\frac{\pi d_h^2}{4}\gtrsim E_{\rm bulk}p^2.
\]
Here $E_h$ and $E_{\rm bulk}$ are the fields inside the hole and in the drift region, respectively, $d_h$ is the hole diameter, and $p$ is the pixel pitch.
For a pitch of 2 mm, a bulk field of 200 V/cm and a hole field of 5 kV/cm, this gives a minimum hole diameter of approximately 0.45 mm. Thus, we take 0.5 mm as a preliminary design parameter. Full flux collection in this simple geometry implies a large ion collection efficiency that we take as one for the time being.


\subsection{Pixel electronics and readout}

The right panel of Fig. \ref{fig:nausicaa} shows a schematic of the electronics. Each pad is directly connected to the input of a CSA. The input current is integrated on a feedback capacitor $C_f$. A MOS transistor allows $C_f$ to discharge slowly. The gain is  $1/C_{f}$, and the signal decays with time constant $\tau_{f}$.


The sampled voltage includes charge accumulated in previous buckets. The charge arriving in each bucket is recovered from the change between successive samples, accounting for feedback discharge. Digitisation is performed at the periphery of the ASIC or on an external carrier, using one or a small number of multiplexed ADCs.

\subsection{Parameters of the design}

In summary, the pad size, pitch and active area must be chosen to sample and contain the ion track. The feedback capacitance and discharge time depend on the charge arriving at each pad and its distribution in time. The sampling interval, electronic noise and ADC range must allow the weaker parts of the track to be recorded while accommodating the largest signals.


\section{Signal formation and response of one pad}
\label{sec:single_ion}

Consider an ion, with charge $q=e$, arriving at a readout pad. The process of signal formation can be viewed in two steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item As the ion approaches, a current is induced in the pad. 
\item Then the CSA integrates it and produces the voltage sampled by the readout.
\end{enumerate}

Next, we describe each step:

\subsection{The induced current}

The weighting potential of a pad, $\phi_{w}(r)$, describes how an ion’s motion contributes to the signal on that pad. We calculate it for the detector geometry with the following boundary conditions: (a) the selected pad is held at unit potential and (b) all other electrodes at zero. Also the calculation is performed in the absence of charge. 

We define positive current as current delivered into the CSA input by an approaching positive ion. With this convention, the meaning of $\phi_w$ follows from the Shockley--Ramo relation~\cite{riegler2016}
\begin{equation}
 \Delta Q=e\,\Delta\phi_w.
 \label{eq:single_charge}
\end{equation}

The current is the rate at which this signal develops:
\begin{equation}
 i_1(t)=e\frac{\dd\phi_w(\mathbf r(t))}{\dd t}.
 \label{eq:single_ramo}
\end{equation}
Near a small pad, $\phi_w$ changes rapidly with position, so most of the signal is induced during the final approach. 

For the analytical calculation we use a square pad of side $2a$, embedded in an infinite grounded plane. The gas occupies a homogeneous half-space above the plane. The ion approaches the centre of the pad along its normal at constant speed $v$. The gap width $g$ is the distance between the lower IFG electrode and the pad plane. This geometry provides a simple pad response. In this simple approximation, the IFG and the dielectric structure of the CMOS surface are not included in its weighting potential. Appendix~\ref{app:single_ion} gives the derivation and boundary conditions.

Place the pad at $z=0$ and take collection to occur at $t=0$, so that $z=-vt$ before collection. The weighting field on the central axis is
%
\begin{equation}
 W(z)=-\frac{\dd\phi_w}{\dd z}
 =\frac{4a^2}{\pi(z^2+a^2)\sqrt{z^2+2a^2}}.
 \label{eq:single_weight}
\end{equation}
%
For an ion entering this gap at $z=g$ and $t_g=-g/v$, the current is
%
\begin{equation}
 i_1(t)=
 \begin{cases}
 evW(-vt),&t_g\leq t<0,\\
 0,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:single_current}
\end{equation}
%
Its integral over this finite trajectory is $Q_g=e[1-\phi_w(g)]$.

\subsection{Signal size and duration}

As a numerical example, we take the parameters shown in the left panel of Fig. \ref{fig:nausicaa}: a pad of side \SI{\SignalPadWidth}{mm} and a gap of \SI{\SignalGap}{mm}. We estimate the gap velocity from $v=\mu E_g$, using the mobility inferred from the bulk drift speed and assuming it remains constant in the gap. With $E_g=\SI{\SignalGapField}{kV/cm}$, this gives $v=\SI{\GapSpeed}{m/s}$. 

The current rises as the ion approaches the pad and reaches
\begin{equation}
 i_1(0^-)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{ev}{a}
 =\SI{\PeakCurrent}{fA}.
 \label{eq:single_peak}
\end{equation}
%
Most of the signal is induced close to the pad (Figure~\ref{fig:single_ion_current}). The final $\sim$\SI{200}{\micro\second} before collection contain 90\% of the full-path charge $e$ \footnote{Here ``full path'' means extending the same analytical trajectory to infinite distance.}. For the finite gap used in the calculation (Appendix~\ref{app:single_duration}),
%
\begin{equation}
 Q_g=e[1-\phi_w(g)]=\GapChargeFraction\,e.
 \label{eq:single_gap_charge}
\end{equation}
%

\subsection{The CSA voltage}

We describe the CSA by an ideal amplifier with feedback capacitance $C_f$ and an effective resistance $R_f$ representing the MOS discharge path. The output voltage $V_1$ is measured relative to its baseline. The input current is shared between the feedback capacitor and the discharge path:
\begin{equation}
 i_1(t)=-C_f\frac{\dd V_1}{\dd t}-\frac{V_1}{R_f}.
 \label{eq:csa_current_balance}
\end{equation}
Where rhe first term describes charge integration and the second feedback discharge. With the discharge time $\tau_f=R_fC_f$, this becomes
%
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd V_1}{\dd t}+\frac{V_1}{\tau_f}
 =-\frac{i_1(t)}{C_f}.
 \label{eq:csa_ode}
\end{equation}
%
Setting $V_1(t_g)=0$, we find the solution 
\begin{equation}
 V_1(t)=-\frac{1}{C_f}\int_{t_g}^{t}
 i_1(t')\exp\left[-\frac{t-t'}{\tau_f}\right]\dd t'.
 \label{eq:csa_solution}
\end{equation}
With this convention positive input current produces a negative voltage. Once collection is complete, the voltage decays exponentially, $V_1(t)=V_1(0)\exp(-t/\tau_f)$.

If discharge is negligible during the pulse, the voltage at collection is $V_1(0)=-Q_g/C_f$. Taking an  illustrative value of $C_f=\SI{\ReadoutCapacitance}{fF}$, the resulting voltage is \SI{\SingleIonIdealVoltage}{\micro\volt}. Figure~\ref{fig:single_ion_response} and Table~\ref{tab:single_ion_response} shows the idealized response of the CSA ---not including substantial effects such as linear response; transistor noise, finite amplifier bandwidth and saturation---

Since the tracks we want to reconstruct produce typically $10^5$ ions distributed over a time of order \SI{1}{\second}, 
a bucket of \SI{10}{\milli\second} contains on average about $10^3$ ions, which could land in the same pad in the limit of a track exactly parallel to the drift lines, neglecting transverse diffusion. The induced current when several ions arrive during a readout frame is the sum of the individual ion-induced currents. For ions following the same final trajectory and collected on the same pad at times $t_k$, the linear model gives
\begin{equation}
 i_p(t)=\sum_k i_1(t-t_k),\qquad
 V_p(t)=\sum_k V_1(t-t_k).
 \label{eq:pad_superposition}
\end{equation}

\section{Beyond the ideal CSA model}
\label{sec:realistic_csa}

The next step is to make our model more realistic. The ideal CSA described in the previous section keeps its input at a fixed potential and responds immediately to an input charge.  Instead, a real amplifier has finite gain and a finite bandwidth. This has two implications. With finite gain, the input voltage is no longer fixed, and the output signal is smaller than the ideal value $Q/C_{f}$. Finite bandwidth limits how quickly the output can respond to the arriving current. Electronic noise also sets a limit on the minimum charge that can be measured. In this section, we extend the model to include these effects. The more realistic CSA model also includes the capacitance of the pad and amplifier input. The feedback circuit consists of a capacitor and a MOS discharge path, represented here by an effective resistance. Our improved model, however, still assumes a linear response. The range of validity of this assumption will be established by our last step, using  SPICE simulations.


\subsection{Finite gain and response time}

\begin{figure}[!htbp] 
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{amplifier_gain.pdf}
 \caption{Normalized open-loop gain of the amplifier, $|A(f)|/A_0=[1+(f/f_a)^2]^{-1/2}$. At low frequencies the gain approaches $A_0$. The marked point is $f=f_a$, where its magnitude is $A_0/\sqrt{2}$.}
 \label{fig:amplifier_gain}
\end{figure}

Define $C_{\rm in}$ as the capacitance between the input node and ground, including the pad, amplifier input and stray capacitances, but excluding $C_f$. Let $A_0$ be the amplifier gain for a constant input. When the input varies rapidly compared with the amplifier's response time, the response is weaker. This can be described by replacing the constant gain with a frequency-dependent gain
%
\begin{equation}
 A(s)=\frac{A_0}{1+s/\omega_a},\qquad V(s)=-A(s)V_{\rm in}(s),
 \label{eq:realistic_gain}
\end{equation}
%
Here $V_{\rm in}(s)$ and $V(s)$ are the Laplace transforms of the input and output voltages. For a sinusoidal component of the input at frequency $f$, we set $s=2\pi\mathrm{i}f$.  Furthermore, $\omega_a=2\pi f_a$, where $f_a$ is the frequency at which the gain magnitude falls to $A_0/\sqrt{2}$.

Notice the limits: at low frequencies, $2\pi f\ll\omega_a$, the denominator is close to one and the gain is approximately $A_0$. As the frequency increases, the denominator reduces the gain. Thus $A_0$ specifies the gain in the limit of slowly varying signals, while $\omega_a$ determines how rapidly the input can change before the response is reduced. This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:amplifier_gain}, which shows that the gain remains close to $A_0$ for frequencies well below $f_a$, falls to $A_0/\sqrt{2}$ at $f=f_a$, and decreases further as the frequency increases. See Appendix~\ref{app:realistic_csa}  for a full derivation. 


The gain $A(s)$ describes how the amplifier converts its input voltage into an output voltage. To obtain the response to the current induced on the pad, we must also include the input capacitance $C_{\rm in}$ and the feedback elements $C_f$ and $R_f$. Part of the input current charges $C_{\rm in}$, while the rest flows through the feedback capacitor and discharge path. Balancing these currents gives $H_I(s)$, the current-to-voltage response of the complete CSA (Appendix~\ref{app:realistic_csa}):
%
\begin{equation}
 H_I(s)\equiv\frac{V(s)}{I(s)}
 =-\frac{A(s)}{sC_{\rm in}+[1+A(s)](R_f^{-1}+sC_f)}.
 \label{eq:realistic_transimpedance}
\end{equation}
%

To isolate the effect of finite gain, we neglect the amplifier response time and feedback discharge. In this approximation, we replace $A(s)$ by $A_0$ and neglect $R_f^{-1}$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:realistic_transimpedance} reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
 H_I(s)=-\frac{1}{s\left[C_f+(C_{\rm in}+C_f)/A_0\right]}.
 \label{eq:realistic_integrating_limit}
\end{equation}
%
For this calculation, we take the start of the input as $t=0$, with zero initial accumulated charge. Taking the Laplace transform gives
%
\begin{equation}
 Q(t)=\int_0^t i(t')\,\dd t'
 \qquad\xleftrightarrow{\;\mathcal{L}\;}\qquad
 Q(s)=\frac{I(s)}{s}.
 \label{eq:realistic_charge_transform}
\end{equation}
%
Thus $I(s)/s$ represents the accumulated charge, and
%
\begin{equation}
 V(s)=H_I(s)I(s)
 =-\frac{I(s)/s}{C_f+(C_{\rm in}+C_f)/A_0}
 =-\frac{Q(s)}{C_f+(C_{\rm in}+C_f)/A_0}.
 \label{eq:realistic_voltage_transform}
\end{equation}
%
Returning to the time domain, the output voltage is
\begin{equation}
 V(t)=-\frac{Q(t)}{C_f+(C_{\rm in}+C_f)/A_0}.
 \label{eq:realistic_charge_gain}
\end{equation}
%
And we recover the ideal value $-Q(t)/C_f$ when  $A_0C_f\gg C_{\rm in}+C_f$. 

\subsection{Several ions in a readout frame}

For k ions following the same trajectory and collected at times $t_j$, the input current is the sum of the single-ion currents calculated in Section~\ref{sec:single_ion}:
%
\begin{equation}
 i_k(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}i_1(t-t_j).
 \label{eq:realistic_packet_current}
\end{equation}
%
The current-to-voltage response derived above then gives
%
\begin{equation}
 V_k(s)=H_I(s)I_k(s).
 \label{eq:realistic_packet_transform}
\end{equation}
%
Returning to the time domain, this product becomes
%
\begin{equation}
 V_k(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}h_I(t-t')i_k(t')\,\dd t',
 \label{eq:realistic_packet}
\end{equation}
%
where $h_I(t)$ is the voltage response per unit charge injected instantaneously at $t=0$.

If no new charge arrived, the voltage would decay from $\overline V_{n-1}$ to $\alpha\overline V_{n-1}$ during one frame of duration $\Delta t$, where $\alpha=e^{-\Delta t/\tau_f}$ and $\tau_f=R_fC_f$. Subtracting this contribution from $\overline V_n$ gives the voltage due to a new charge arriving during the frame.

\[
\overline V_n-\alpha\overline V_{n-1}.
\]

We take the current $I_n=Q_n/\Delta t$ as constant during the frame and measure the voltage at its beginning and end. Applying the ideal CSA solution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:csa_solution} gives
%
\begin{equation}
 \overline V_n-\alpha\overline V_{n-1}
 =-\frac{I_n}{C_f}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_n}
 e^{-(t_n-t')/\tau_f}\,\dd t'.
 \label{eq:realistic_frame_integral}
\end{equation}
%
The exponential accounts for discharge between the arrival time $t'$ and the sample time $t_n$. Evaluating the integral gives
\begin{equation}
 \overline V_n-\alpha\overline V_{n-1}
 =-\frac{I_n\tau_f}{C_f}(1-\alpha)
 =-\frac{Q_n}{C_f}\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta t/\tau_f}.
 \label{eq:realistic_frame_increment}
\end{equation}
Moving the previous-frame contribution to the right gives
\begin{equation}
 \overline V_n=\alpha\overline V_{n-1}
 -\frac{Q_n}{C_f}\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta t/\tau_f}.
 \label{eq:realistic_frame_voltage}
\end{equation}
Solving for the charge gives
%
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Q_n=-K\left(\overline V_n-\alpha\overline V_{n-1}\right),\qquad
 K=C_f\frac{\Delta t/\tau_f}{1-\alpha},
 \label{eq:realistic_estimator}
\end{equation}
%
We can now calculate the voltages $\overline V_n$ using the CSA response $H_I(s)$ obtained previously (Eq.~\eqref{eq:realistic_transimpedance}) and insert them into Eq.~\eqref{eq:realistic_estimator} to obtain $\widehat Q_n$. This allows us to compare this estimate with the input charge $Q_n=\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_n}i_k(t)\,\dd t$, and from there assess how accurately we recover the charge.

\subsection{Noise in the recovered charge}
\label{sec:charge_noise}

We now need to compare the recovered charge with the electronic noise. The amplifier and the feedback discharge path produce voltage fluctuations even when no ions arrive. We describe their effect by a voltage noise source and a current noise source at the amplifier input, which we assume to be independent~\cite{tiNoise2007,spielerNoise1998}. The voltage noise includes a white component ---equal contributions to the variance per unit frequency---, and a flicker component, whose spectrum varies as $1/f$. The current noise includes the contribution from the feedback path. Appendix~\ref{app:realistic_noise} shows how the circuit response converts these sources into output voltage noise.

Let $\delta V_n$ be the noise in the voltage measured at the end of frame $n$. Inserting the two voltage fluctuations into the charge estimator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:realistic_estimator} gives
\begin{equation}
 \delta\widehat Q_n=-K\left(\delta V_n-\alpha\delta V_{n-1}\right).
 \label{eq:realistic_charge_fluctuation}
\end{equation}
Thus the uncertainty in the recovered charge depends on the noise in both readings. Positively correlated fluctuations partly cancel when we take the difference between readings. For simplicity, assume that the noise has zero mean and the same variance at each reading. Define the variance of one reading as
\begin{equation}
 C_V(0)=\left\langle\delta V_n^2\right\rangle,
 \label{eq:realistic_sample_variance}
\end{equation}
and the covariance between consecutive readings as
\begin{equation}
 C_V(\Delta t)=\left\langle\delta V_n\delta V_{n-1}\right\rangle.
 \label{eq:realistic_sample_covariance}
\end{equation}
Squaring Eq.~\eqref{eq:realistic_charge_fluctuation} and taking the average gives
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{\widehat Q}^{2}
 =K^2\left[(1+\alpha^2)C_V(0)-2\alpha C_V(\Delta t)\right].
 \label{eq:realistic_noise_charge}
\end{equation}
For uncorrelated readings, $C_V(\Delta t)=0$. Notice also that a positive covariance reduces the noise in the charge estimate. The two quantities are calculated from the output noise spectrum as described in Appendix~\ref{app:realistic_noise}.

Let $g_n$ be the mean estimated charge per ion in frame $n$. For $k$ ions, the estimated charge is $kg_n$, while the electronic noise has standard deviation $\sigma_{\widehat Q}$. The signal-to-noise ratio and the number of ions needed to reach a ratio $\rho$ are then
\begin{equation}
 {\rm S/N}_n(k)=\frac{k|g_n|}{\sigma_{\widehat Q}},\qquad
 k_{\min,n}=\frac{\rho\,\sigma_{\widehat Q}}{|g_n|}.
 \label{eq:realistic_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

\section{Analytical single-ion response}
\label{app:single_ion}

\subsection{Weighting potential of a square pad}

The pad occupies $-a<x<a$, $-a<y<a$ in the plane $z=0$. Its weighting potential is one, and the remainder of the plane is held at zero. The upper half-space has uniform permittivity and no free charge in the weighting problem. The potential tends to zero at infinity. Thus $\nabla^2\phi_w=0$ for $z>0$, with a prescribed potential on the boundary.

The half-space Poisson kernel gives~\cite{salazar2021}
\begin{equation}
 \phi_w(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-a}^{a}\int_{-a}^{a}
 \frac{z\,\dd x'\,\dd y'}{[(x-x')^2+(y-y')^2+z^2]^{3/2}}.
 \label{eq:app_poisson}
\end{equation}
The integral is the solid angle subtended by the pad, divided by $2\pi$. On the central axis, integration over the square yields
\begin{equation}
 \phi_w(z)=\frac{2}{\pi}\tan^{-1}
 \left[\frac{a^2}{z\sqrt{z^2+2a^2}}\right],\qquad z>0.
 \label{eq:app_square_phi}
\end{equation}
It approaches one at the pad and zero far from it. Differentiation gives the field in Eq.~\eqref{eq:single_weight}. At large distance,
\begin{equation}
 \phi_w(z)\simeq\frac{2a^2}{\pi z^2},\qquad
 W(z)\simeq\frac{4a^2}{\pi z^3}.
\end{equation}
The weighting field has units of inverse length because $\phi_w$ is dimensionless.

This boundary condition represents a metal pad surrounded by grounded metal. An exposed dielectric surface between pads would require a different solution. The actual IFG also modifies the weighting potential. The present expression is used as an analytical reference, with fixed electrode potentials and negligible perturbation of the physical field by the ion charge. Resistive materials can require time-dependent weighting fields~\cite{riegler2016,riegler2019}.

\subsection{Trajectory, charge and duration}
\label{app:single_duration}

The bulk operating point gives the mobility
\begin{equation}
 \mu=\frac{v_d}{E_{\rm bulk}}
 =\Mobility\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/(V\,s)}.
\end{equation}
We use the same mobility in the nominal uniform gap field, giving $v=\mu E_g$. Diffusion during the final approach is omitted from the single-ion reference trajectory. The ion moves from $z=g$ to zero at this constant speed. Its induced current is Eq.~\eqref{eq:single_current}, and direct integration gives
\begin{equation}
 \int_{-g/v}^{0}i_1(t)\dd t
 =e\int_0^g W(z)\dd z
 =e[1-\phi_w(g)].
\end{equation}
For the example in Section~\ref{sec:single_ion}, $\phi_w(g)=\GapPotential$. The finite-path signal is not rescaled to $e$. Extending this trajectory to infinite distance gives $\int i_1\dd t=e$, but does not model passage through the IFG.

Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_square_phi} gives
\begin{equation}
 z(\phi)=a\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sin(\pi\phi/2)}-1}.
 \label{eq:app_inverse_phi}
\end{equation}
For the full reference trajectory, the interval immediately preceding collection that contains a fraction $F$ of the charge has duration
\begin{equation}
 T_F=\frac{z(1-F)}{v}.
\end{equation}
For a finite starting height $g$, the corresponding duration is
\begin{equation}
 T_{F,g}=\frac{z\!\left(1-F[1-\phi_w(g)]\right)}{v}.
\end{equation}
These definitions refer to integrated charge, not to the time above a fraction of the peak current. The pulse amplitude scales as $ev/a$, and its characteristic duration as $a/v$. Their product preserves the full-path charge.

For comparison, a circular pad of radius $a$ in the same grounded plane has
\begin{equation}
 \phi_{w,\circ}(z)=1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+a^2}},\qquad
 W_\circ(z)=\frac{a^2}{(z^2+a^2)^{3/2}}.
\end{equation}
Its central peak is $ev/a=\SI{\DiskPeakCurrent}{fA}$ for the same lateral diameter as the square side, compared with \SI{\PeakCurrent}{fA} for the square. Both results assume the same constant velocity and grounded surroundings. Their difference illustrates shape dependence; it does not quantify the uncertainty of the real IFG--pad structure.

\subsection{Integration and feedback discharge}

For an ideal CSA, the input node remains at its reference potential. Current balance through the parallel feedback elements gives
\begin{equation}
 C_f\frac{\dd V_1}{\dd t}+\frac{V_1}{R_f}=-i_1(t).
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $\exp(t/\tau_f)$ and integration from $t_g=-g/v$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:csa_solution}. In the absence of discharge,
\begin{equation}
 V_1(t)=-\frac{e}{C_f}[\phi_w(-vt)-\phi_w(g)],
 \qquad t_g\leq t\leq0.
\end{equation}
The voltage remains at $-Q_g/C_f$ after collection. With finite $\tau_f$, its value at collection can also be written as
\begin{equation}
 V_1(0)=-\frac{e}{C_f}\int_0^g W(z)
 \exp\left(-\frac{z}{v\tau_f}\right)\dd z.
 \label{eq:app_csa_quadrature}
\end{equation}
This form permits an independent numerical check of the time-domain calculation.

Integrating the CSA equation between two readout times $t_{n-1}$ and $t_n$ gives the exact charge relation
\begin{equation}
 Q_n=-C_f\left[V_n-V_{n-1}
 +\frac{1}{\tau_f}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_n}V(t)\dd t\right],
 \label{eq:app_bucket_charge}
\end{equation}
where $Q_n$ is the induced input charge in that interval and $V_n=V(t_n)$. Thus the difference between samples gives the charge when discharge is negligible. With finite discharge, the correction depends on the voltage between samples. Recovering it from discrete samples requires a model or approximation for that interval.

If the current is constant over an interval $\Delta t$, its charge and the sampled voltages satisfy
\begin{equation}
 Q_n=-C_f\frac{\Delta t/\tau_f}{1-\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_f)}
 \left[V_n-\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_f)V_{n-1}\right].
 \label{eq:app_bucket_constant}
\end{equation}
The limit $\tau_f\to\infty$ is $Q_n=-C_f(V_n-V_{n-1})$. Equation~\eqref{eq:app_bucket_constant} assumes constant current within the interval; two endpoint samples do not otherwise determine an arbitrary arrival history.

\subsection{Numerical evaluation and checks}

The current is represented by charge increments rather than point samples. For a time bin $[t_j,t_{j+1}]$ during the approach,
\begin{equation}
 q_j=e[\phi_w(-vt_{j+1})-\phi_w(-vt_j)].
\end{equation}
These increments sum exactly to the finite-path charge. Taking the bin-average current $q_j/\delta t$ gives the recurrence
\begin{equation}
 V_{j+1}=\alpha V_j
 -\frac{q_j}{C_f}\frac{\tau_f}{\delta t}(1-\alpha),
 \qquad \alpha=\exp(-\delta t/\tau_f).
 \label{eq:app_recurrence}
\end{equation}
This is exact for a constant current in each numerical bin and converges to the continuous-current solution as $\delta t$ decreases. The numerical step is separate from the readout sampling interval.

The square-pad potential is checked against independent surface integration of Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_poisson}, and its derivative against Eq.~\eqref{eq:single_weight}. Charge integrals, boundary limits and scaling with pad size and velocity are also checked. The CSA response is compared with Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_csa_quadrature}, the ideal-integrator limit and the closed-form response to a constant current. These checks test the analytical model and its numerical implementation; they do not replace a field calculation for the collection geometry or a transistor-level circuit calculation.
